Dirichlet Noise

Dirichlet noise, introduced by AlphaGo Zero (2017), is a common way of improving the exploration of the MCTS simulations. It adds some noise to the policy head, $P(s,a)$, when it is evaluated at the root node (i.e. only the first move, not deeper moves). Dirichlet noise is similar to temperature in that they both add some randomness to the game, but they have different purposes:

  • Dirichlet noise adds randomness to the search, forcing it to consider moves it would normally under-explore.
  • Temperature has no impact on the search, and only adds randomness to the move selection. A move with very low visit count is almost never selected, even with moderate temperature.

In other words, the methods are highly complementary: Dirichlet noise shakes up the search, while temperature shakes up the moves. The noisy policy head used at the root node is defined as $$P_\mathrm{Dir}(s,a) = P(s,a)[1-\epsilon] + \epsilon \eta(a),$$ where $P(s,a)$ is the "normal" policy, $\eta(a)$ is some noise across the different moves $a$, and $\epsilon\in [0,1]$ is just some parameter saying what fraction of the new policy should be noise, typically set to something like 0.25.

Note that, unlike temperature, Dirichlet noise is only used during self-play training, not during "real" play. It exists to make the network learn new moves, while temperature is also used to create varied moves during real play.

The added noise, $\eta(a)$ is drawn from a Dirichlet distribution, $\eta(a) \sim \text{Dir}(\alpha)$. When the parameter $\alpha$ is set lower than 1, the Dirichlet distribution produces random numbers that are highly concentrated in a few of the elements of $\eta$. See the interactive plot below.

Using more uniform noise (i.e. $\alpha \geq 1$) would just dilute the often very limited number of simulations we have at our disposal across a wide range of different moves. By using a highly "spiked" noise we concentrate our resources on testing one or a few moves that the policy would not choose to explore. Note that if these moves turn out to be blunders, the $Q$-value of those moves will drop, and the search will go back to exploring other moves.

To avoid this dilution, $\alpha$ is usually set to something around 0.1 times the typical number of legal moves in a game. AlphaZero uses $\alpha=0.3$ for Chess, 0.15 for Shoki, and 0.03 for Go, all approximately following this rule of thumb. Brandubh falls somewhere around Chess in terms of legal moves, with a slightly smaller board and fewer pieces, but with each piece typically having more moves. A value around 0.3 would probably work well for Brandubh too.

Dirichlet noise will slightly dilute the policy training target with bad moves, but the tradeoff is typically deemed worth it, and the value head is unaffectedDemonstrating yet another advantage of having both a policy and a value head: The policy head can afford to be more exploratory and experimental when the value head is kept as a more pristine grounding.. The upside is also huge: A policy network that has found a set of moves that works well will dedicate little time to exploration without something like Dirichlet noise, and the temperature won't select moves that the search refused to explore at all. A mechanism like Dirichlet noise is important for learning more complicated tactics at any sensible rate of efficiency.

When Dirichlet noise becomes too diluting

For games with very high branching factors, like Go, the dilution of the policy training target from the Dirichlet noise can get strong enough to impair training. KataGo partially mitigated this by keeping track of the extra visits imposed by the noise, and only keeping a fraction of them depending on how promising the moves look after-the-fact, judged by their $Q$-values.
